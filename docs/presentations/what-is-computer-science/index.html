Everything that follows is built from one distinction: the difference between a proof and the truth.

Those two colours keep those two jobs for the whole half hour: proof, notation, syntax — truth, meaning, semantics.

Full disclosure

Open with the disclosure and let the laugh happen, then move on; it is a fact about how the deck was made, not a point to be argued. The question

The name misleads twice. It is not about computers, and it is only sometimes a science.

What it studies is older than both: notation — what can be written down, and what the marks can and cannot do once they are written.

It met that question where a machine forces the notation to be exact. That is why the limits were noticed here first, and measured here most sharply — and why they hold everywhere else.

So the answer to the title is a definition, stated at the end and earned first. Nothing in the derivation depends on which computer, which language, or which year.

Computer science is no more about computers than astronomy is about telescopes. attributed to Edsger W. Dijkstra

The short answer, to be earned: computer science is the exact study of the gap between notation and meaning.

Put the shape of the answer on the table in the first two minutes. Two corrections to the name: the machine is the instrument, not the subject — hence the telescope line, which everybody attributes to Dijkstra and nobody has found in his writing, so say "attributed" — and the discipline is part mathematics, part engineering, and only sometimes an empirical science. Then the actual subject: notation, and the fact that a machine will do exactly what the marks say and nothing else, which is the one place where the difference between what a text says and what it means cannot be papered over. Give the short answer and let it sit; the six parts are its derivation.

Part I

Size

First a feeling for how big the spaces are that programs live in — because everything later, including every bug and every AI, happens inside one.

Part I is arithmetic only. No theorems. The goal is a physical feeling for state space, because a number you cannot picture is a number you cannot argue with, and every later claim is a claim about where something sits on one line.

The map

One line carries the whole talk: small, vast, then two sizes of endless.

log scale · then off the end of every scale

Left. From one thing to every atom there is — the whole physical world in eighty steps, each one ten times the last.

The wall. No number of steps crosses it. Infinity is not the far end of the line; it is what the line never reaches.

Right. Past the wall, endlessness comes in sizes: one counts every notation, the other every meaning — and is strictly bigger.

The itinerary of the lecture in one picture. Walk the sweep left to right: everything physics has to offer sits inside those eighty decades. Then the wall, and the sentence to land: infinity is not a very large number, it is not on the line at all, which is why Part II needs an argument rather than a bigger example. Then the two boxes, countable and uncountable, drawn at different sizes on purpose. Do not prove anything here, just plant the shape. Parts I and IV are about the left of this line, Parts II and III about the right.

One bit

A bit is one distinction. Add one and you double the states — so 34 bytes beat the universe.

Ten bits: a thousand states. Twenty: a million. Thirty: a billion. Doubling is the most underestimated operation in human reasoning — and the entire engine of computing.

1080atoms in the observable universe
2266states of 266 bits ≈ 1.2 × 1080
log scale · 1 → 10⁸⁰

Ask the room to guess how many bits it takes to have more states than the universe has atoms; answers land in the millions or billions. Then the ruler: 266 bits, thirty-four bytes, one short sentence, one cryptographic key, already exceeds the atom count of everything we can see. That is why cryptography works at all, and the first hint that the spaces we build inside computers are not merely large but of a different order than physical reality.

A system you can read · on a machine you cannot

Selfie is 12,394 lines. Its machine has 234,359,738,368 states.

Selfie is the specimen this talk uses: one file of C, containing a compiler, an emulator and a hypervisor, each of which is applied to itself. Small enough to read to the end — which turns out to be the only way a meaning is ever actually pinned down.

The machine it runs on has 4 GB of memory — 235 bits. Written out in decimal, its state count has about 10.3 billion digits: 3.4 million pages, some 6,900 volumes of 500 pages. That shelf does not hold the states. It holds the number of them, written down once.

Every program selfie will ever run, and every bug in any of them, is one point in that space.

$ ./selfie -c selfie.c selfie compiling selfie.c to 64-bit RISC-U with 64-bit starc 365784 characters read in 12394 lines and 1741 comments 491 global variables, 661 procedures, 512 string literals 188392 bytes generated with 43492 instructions

Introduce the specimen here, in the middle of an argument about size, because the contrast is the point. Every number in the terminal is selfie reporting on selfie: the compiler counted its own source and told you. Twelve thousand lines is a system one person can finish reading, and that is its whole design. Then the other number. Memory is base two, so 4 GB is 235 bits and the state count is 2 to that power — a number whose decimal expansion alone fills a small library. There is no physical analogy left; the universe's atom count is an 81-digit number and this is a ten-billion-digit one. A system you can read completely runs on a space nobody can inspect, and that gap is where the rest of the talk lives.

The consequence

In a space that big, good states and bad states look alike.

tested states, lit

Test a billion states per second, starting at the Big Bang, and by now you would have checked about 288 of them — out of 234,359,738,368.

The fraction is not small. It is indistinguishable from zero.

Testing shows the presence, not the absence of bugs.Edsger W. Dijkstra, 1969

So knowing what a program does cannot be exhaustive search. It has to be argument, structure, abstraction — which is to say, notation. That is where Part III begins.

This reframes bugs: they are not evidence of sloppy people, they are the geometry of the space. No diligence lets you visit the states. Say the consequence for Part V already, in one sentence: "we tested it extensively" is this slide, whoever or whatever wrote the code. Testing shows presence, not absence, and that is why the subject is built on argument and abstraction rather than on trying things.

Part II

Infinity

Vast is still finite. Now the things that are not — and the discovery that endlessness comes in two sizes.

Warn the room: this is the only genuinely mathematical stretch, and it is the load-bearing one. One slide is a worked proof and it is worth the minute. Everything after — Gödel, Turing, hallucination — is this one argument wearing different clothes.

Notation

Everything you can write down is countable.

Two sets are the same size if you can pair them up, one to one, nothing left over. A set pairable with 1, 2, 3, … is countable: it can be listed.

A program is a finite string of symbols. So is a proof, a specification, a prompt. List all strings of length 1, then 2, then 3 … every finite text appears at some finite position.

So all programs that will ever exist form one list. Same for all proofs. Same for all prompts anyone will ever type.

the enumeration of all texts
This has a name

Giving each piece of text a number is Gödelisierung — Gödel numbering. selfie.c is 365,784 characters: one string, one very long number. It is how a machine reads a machine, and Part III is built on it.

Two moves. First the definition of "same size" without counting, and the word countable, which means listable — first, second, third — and does all the work in this Part. Then the key observation: notation is countable, not because notation is small but because it is finite and discrete. The audience should feel this as generous; countable is already infinite. Name Gödelisierung here and make it concrete with selfie's own source: a file is a string, a string is a number, and a compiler is a program that eats programs.

The proof

Hand me a list of every behaviour. I will build one that is not on it.

● yes · ○ no

A behaviour is an endless answer sheet: for input 1, yes or no; for input 2, yes or no; forever. Every infinite bit string is one. Suppose they could be listed: S1, S2, S3, …, every behaviour somewhere on the list.

Go down the diagonal and ask each row about its own number. What does S1 say about 1? S2 about 2? Sn about n?

Build D by answering the opposite every time: D(n) = not Sn(n). Nothing exotic — a rule anyone can apply, one number at a time.

D is not S1: they disagree about 1. Not S2: they disagree about 2. Not Sn, for any n. So the list left something out — and it was any list at all. Cantor, 1891.

Take a full minute. Build the grid, ring the diagonal, then construct D one column at a time out loud. Then the kill: ask the room where D is on the list. Somebody will say "further down" — point at row n and ask what D and Sn say about the number n. They disagree by construction, at exactly one place, and that is enough. No assumption was made about the list, so every list fails, so no list exists. Name the pattern now because it recurs three more times in Part III: assume a complete list, ask each item about itself, answer the opposite. Cantor, Gödel, Turing, Rice — one move.

The result everything hangs on

There are incomparably more meanings than notations.

countable notation · uncountable meaning

Programs: countable. Behaviours: uncountable. So almost every behaviour has no program.

Proofs: countable. Truths about numbers: uncountable. So almost every truth has no proof.

Prompts: countable. Things you might mean by one: uncountable. So almost everything is unsaid.

Turn it over

No vocabulary — mathematical, legal, neural — will ever cover the space of meanings. Every new notation captures meaning that was unreachable before, and there is always more left. The supply never runs out.

The thesis slide. Say it three ways, slowly, and note that no logic or computability has been used yet: pure counting already tells us notation can never catch up with meaning. Gödel and Turing will only make it concrete. Then turn it over: scarcity of notation is not a wall, it is unclaimed territory. Calculus, the periodic table, type systems — each one a raid on the uncountable, and the uncountable does not shrink.

Part III

Self-Reference

Counting says the gap exists. Self-reference walks to the edge and points — and the same step, taken forwards, builds the computer.

Part II was static, a size comparison. Part III is constructive: we will write down specific questions no notation can answer, and then watch the identical move produce the universal machine. The instrument is always a system turned on itself, and selfie is that instrument made runnable.

Syntax and semantics

A compiler defines the meaning of the language it is written in.

What does while mean? Not what the manual says. What it means is whatever the compiler emits — a comparison and a branch — and whatever the machine then does with those.

So the meaning of a program is fixed by another program. And selfie's compiler, starc, is written in the very language it gives meaning to.

An English dictionary written in English. Only paradoxical if you demand that the definitions come first. They do not: the machine comes first, and the dictionary is bootstrapped onto it.

notation → meaning
The distinction, in the room's own terms

Syntax is the text — finite, checkable, copyable. Semantics is what the machine does with it — over all inputs, forever. A semantics is a function from the first to the second, and pinning that function down is the founding act of every exact discipline.

Most people arrive believing a language is defined by a document and the compiler implements the document. For every language anyone actually uses it is the other way round: the document describes, sometimes inaccurately, what an implementation does. Meaning lives in the tool. Selfie makes that literal — you can read the procedure that decides what a while loop means, and it is forty lines. Then set the distinction explicitly, because the whole difficulty of the subject is that the second column is not determined by staring harder at the first.

Gödelisierung · you can run it

Three commands. Three kinds of self-reference.

// self-compilation: the compiler compiles its own source — twice — and the results agree $ ./selfie -c selfie.c -o selfie1.m -m 2 -c selfie.c -o selfie2.m $ diff -s selfie1.m selfie2.m Files selfie1.m and selfie2.m are identical
// self-execution: the emulator executes its own machine code $ ./selfie -c selfie.c -o selfie.m -m 2 -l selfie.m -m 1
// self-hosting: the hypervisor hosts a virtual machine running the hypervisor $ ./selfie -c selfie.c -o selfie.m -m 3 -l selfie.m -y 2 -l selfie.m -y 1

The first is Gödel's move: a text handed to itself. The second is Turing's: a machine handed its own description. The third is what an operating system is. All three run on a laptop.

These are real commands and the point of showing them is that the theorems about to follow are not exotic: they are the invoice for expressiveness that every computer already pays. Read the first one slowly. Selfie compiles its own source to machine code, runs that machine code, has it compile the same source again, and the two results are equal byte for byte — a fixed point, the moment the system stops depending on anything outside itself. The second loads the emulator into the emulator; the inner one cannot tell. The third stacks a hypervisor inside a virtual machine the hypervisor created. Then the mapping to Part III: self-compilation is Gödel numbering made executable, self-execution is the universal machine, and the next four slides say exactly what such loops can and cannot decide. The long version of these three commands is a companion talk of its own.

Theorem

There are truths with no proof — and no strong system can certify itself.

Gödel · incompleteness · 1931
Any consistent formal system rich enough to describe arithmetic contains statements that are true but not provable within it. And it cannot prove its own consistency.
How · the same diagonal

By Gödelisierung, build a sentence that says "this sentence has no proof in this system." If it were provable, the system proves a falsehood. So it is unprovable — and therefore true. Adding it as an axiom does not help: the construction runs again.

Read it as engineering · Thompson 1984

Selfie's fixed point proves the compiler agrees with itself. It cannot prove the binary does what the source says: a compiler can carry a back door that reinserts itself on every self-compilation, invisible in clean source. The check has to come from outside — a second, independent compiler.

Proof is a finite object you can check. Truth is not. Proof is syntax. Truth is semantics. They are not the same size — and nothing certifies itself.

The heart of the talk, in one slide with two theorems. Note what is not claimed: no single statement is forever unknowable; you may prove it in a stronger system. What is claimed is that no system is ever finished, and no system is the source of its own trust. Then the engineering reading, which lands harder in a computer science room than any philosophy: Ken Thompson's Turing Award lecture. Teach a compiler to recognise the login program and insert a back door; teach it to recognise itself and reinsert both tricks; delete both from the source. Every future compiler built from the clean source carries the back door and no reading finds it — self-compilation is how it survives. Wheeler's way out is diverse double-compiling: a different compiler, from outside. Same shape as Gödel's second theorem, and plant it here deliberately: Part V stands on it.

Undecidability

Will this program ever stop? No program can always tell.

feed the decider its own description

Suppose H(P,x) decides, for every program and input, whether P halts on x.

Build D(P): ask H whether P halts on P — then do the opposite.

Now run D(D). It halts exactly if it doesn't. Contradiction. So H never existed.

Turing, 1936 — the same paper that defined the universal machine, and thus invented the computer. The limit and the machine arrived together.

Walk the animation: H, then the wrapper D, then the self-application. Emphasise the historical detail — Turing was answering Hilbert's decision problem in the negative, and the by-product of the negative answer was the design of the machine on every desk. Limits are where technology comes from. Do not land the gloom: the very next slide turns the same move over and shows what it builds.

The same move, forwards

One machine that can be any machine.

the job moved from the wiring to the tape

A machine used to be its job. Turing's move: put the machine's description on the tape, as data. Then one machine U reads any description and does whatever that machine would do. That is universality: one piece of hardware, every possible behaviour, because the behaviour arrives as notation.

Your phone is not phone-shaped. It is U holding a description — which is why a new app needs no new hardware.

Selfie's U is a page of C per instruction group

mipster reads a word of RISC-U code, does what it says, and advances. Fourteen instructions, and it runs every program that will ever exist for that machine — including itself. That is the second of the three commands.

One sentence, read twice

Hand a decider its own description: contradiction, no H. Hand a machine any description: every program at once, one U. Turing published both in 1936.

The slide that stops Part III reading as a funeral. The move that produced the contradiction — a machine handed a description of a machine — is the identical move that produces the computer. Say what universality buys in the room's own terms: nobody here owns a calculator, a typewriter, a map and a record player, because they own one object that becomes any of those when handed a description. Then make it concrete with mipster: an interpreter for fourteen instructions, about a page of code per group, and it is a universal machine — any universal machine can simulate any other, which is why "computable" does not depend on which machine you picked. Universality is also exactly what makes the next slide bite: a machine that can be any machine can have any behaviour, so there is nothing general left to decide.

The general case

It is not just halting. It is every interesting question about meaning.

Rice · 1953
Every non-trivial property of the behaviour of programs is undecidable. Only questions about their text are safe.
Decidable · about syntax

Does it parse? How long is it? Which procedures does it call? Are the types consistent? Does it contain a loop? A compiler answers all of these, always, in finite time.

Undecidable · about semantics

Does it terminate? Is it equivalent to that one? Can it divide by zero? Does it leak the key? Is it correct? No program decides any of these for all programs — whoever, or whatever, wrote them.

So every useful tool — a type checker, a test suite, a fuzzer, a model checker, a proof assistant — is a deliberate approximation: sound but incomplete, or complete but unsound, or exact only within a bound. Choosing which to give up is the discipline. Selfie ships several such choices, applied to selfie.

Rice generalises Turing and is the single most useful theorem for reasoning about any program, generated or written: "is this program correct" is a semantic property, there is no general decision procedure, and the theorem does not care who wrote the program. Frame it as the start of something rather than the end: it says in advance there is no perfect analyser, so the interesting question is never "can we decide this" but "which approximation do we choose and what do we give up". Every real tool is an answer to that question, and Part IV shows what the answers cost.

Part IV

Cost

Suppose a question is decidable. You still have to pay for the answer — in time, space, and energy.

Shift register: Parts II and III were about the possible. Part IV is about the affordable — and for practical purposes the boundary between impossible and unaffordable hardly matters. This is also where physics enters the talk.

Decidable ≠ doable

A question with a guaranteed answer you will never receive.

Given a logical formula over 100 yes/no variables: is there an assignment making it true? Perfectly decidable — try all 2100.

2100≈ 1.3 × 1030 assignments
1013 yearsat a billion checks per second
Where selfie meets it

monster and rotor translate RISC-U machine code — including all of selfie — into exactly such formulae, satisfiable when some input makes the code fail. Syntax becomes semantics, and then hits this wall, before the coffee gets cold.

2ⁿ branches · one satisfying leaf

Note that modern solvers routinely handle millions of variables on structured industrial instances — the exponential is a worst case, and defeating typical cases is one of engineering's great quiet victories. Then the selfie tools: take a program, which is syntax, and its execution, which is semantics, and produce a logical formula whose satisfiability answers a semantic question exactly — within a bound, which is the approximation Rice forces. The formula goes to a solver, and satisfiability is the canonical hard problem, so a laptop, a small C program and twenty minutes get you to the frontier of complexity theory.

Intractability

Hard to find. Easy to check.

That asymmetry has a name: NP — problems whose solutions are quick to verify even if finding them seems to need exponential search.

Cook and Levin, 1971–73: thousands of such problems are the same problem in disguise. Crack one efficiently and you crack scheduling, routing, folding, packing, proving.

Whether that is possible — P = NP? — is the most consequential open question in the exact sciences. Most researchers bet no.

The asymmetry, everywhere

Writing a proof versus reading it. Designing a protein versus assaying it. Writing the program versus running the test. Doing the homework versus grading it.

The asymmetry, named

Generation is expensive; verification is cheap. Every division of labour that works — between people, or between a person and a machine — sits on that gap. Part V comes back to it.

Do not rush it: this asymmetry is the one piece of Part IV that Part V needs. Finding a solution and checking one are different problems, and for a large class of questions the second is cheap while the first appears not to be. That is a fact about problems, not about people; it is why a proof can be read in an afternoon that took a decade to find, and why a division of labour between a producer and a checker is possible at all.

Physics

Computation is not abstract. It costs energy, by law.

joules · log scale

Landauer, 1961: erasing one bit at room temperature costs at least kT ln 2 ≈ 3 × 10-21 joules — because erasing is irreversible: two states become one, and the lost distinction leaves as heat. Information is physical.

So brute-forcing our 266-bit space costs ≈ 1059 J — about 1015 times everything the Sun will radiate in its entire lifetime.

Meanwhile a human brain runs on 20 watts: a dim light bulb, doing what data centres cannot.

Time, space, energy — three currencies for one budget. Any claim about intelligence that ignores the budget is a claim about magic.

The slide that makes "afforded" physical, and it needs four beats. First, why erasure costs anything at all. A bit is one distinction, Part I's definition; erasing it means two possible states of the memory collapse into one, so the memory's entropy falls by k ln 2, and the second law says that entropy cannot vanish — it moves into the surroundings as heat, at least kT ln 2 of it, where k is Boltzmann's constant and T the temperature. At room temperature that is about 3 × 10⁻²¹ joules, Landauer's floor. Say what it is not: not a fact about silicon or about today's engineering, which sits several orders of magnitude above it, but a floor for any physical device whatsoever, because it comes from thermodynamics and not from the design. Bennett showed in 1973 that a computation which never discards information can in principle dodge it, which is a fascinating loophole and does not help here: a search that visits a state and moves on has to forget the last one. Second, the arithmetic. The 266-bit space from Part I has 2²⁶⁶ ≈ 10⁸⁰ states; visiting each one discards at least a bit, so exhaustive search costs at least 10⁸⁰ × 3 × 10⁻²¹ ≈ 3 × 10⁵⁹ joules. The Sun radiates about 4 × 10²⁶ watts and will do so for about ten billion years, roughly 10⁴⁴ joules in total — so the search costs a few thousand trillion suns, at the thermodynamic floor, on a perfect machine. That is the sentence to land: brute force over a 34-byte space is not slow, it is unaffordable by the physics of the universe, and no better hardware changes the exponent. Third, walk the bars. The figure is a log scale, so distances are exponents: one bit erased at 10⁻²⁰; a brain for one day at 10⁶, which is 20 watts times 86,400 seconds; a large training run at about 10¹³, some gigawatt-hours; the Sun's lifetime at 10⁴⁴; and the brute-force bar fifteen decades further out, past the last thing the universe has to offer. Fourth, the brain. Twenty watts is the point, not a flourish: an existence proof that whatever it is doing, it is doing at a price the data centre is nowhere near, which means the current price of computation is not the floor either. Then the last line, which is a statement of method rather than an opinion: time, space and energy are three currencies for one budget, and a description of intelligence — natural or artificial — that leaves the budget out has described a machine that pays for nothing, which is to say magic. Bremermann's limit, 1962, is the general form of the ceiling: about 10⁵⁰ bits per second per kilogram of matter, the most any physical system can compute.

Part V

Machines

Now the machine everyone is asking about. It is a computation — so it is subject to every single thing we have just established.

Tone matters. Not debunking, not hype. These systems are among the most useful artefacts ever built. And they are computations, so the mathematics applies to them exactly as it applies to a spreadsheet.

What it is

A large language model is a machine trained on notation, asked for meaning.

tokens in · a distribution over the next one out · then a sample

The name is exact. What a large language model — an LLM — is trained on is language, so the signal is syntactic: which symbol follows which. Meaning is never handed to it.

That this works as well as it does is the genuine surprise of the decade. It cost megawatts for weeks; you run on twenty watts. Neither figure changes what kind of object it is.

Billions of parameters, each many bits. Part I applies unchanged: that space cannot be inspected, so the behaviour cannot be enumerated. Only sampled.

Keep the description mechanical and unpolemical — the room should leave able to explain what these systems are to a friend. The important consequence is the last one: "we tested it extensively" is Part I all over again. Testing shows presence, not absence.

The old gap, industrialised

A hallucination is a proof-shaped object that isn't true.

three tests · and they are not the same test

Fluency is syntax. Correctness is semantics. We built a machine of extraordinary fluency, so the gap between the two is something you now meet before breakfast.

Not a defect to be patched away: it is the proof/truth distinction at consumer scale. Generated code compiles, reads well, and is wrong in the same way a proof-shaped paragraph is wrong.

So the only thing that settles it is a check against something with a semantics — a compiler, a test, a proof, a measurement, an experiment.

Where the semantics comes from

Not from the model: it was trained on symbols defined by other symbols. From outside it. Part III already said the check is always external — Gödel's stronger system, Thompson's second compiler. Here it is, at consumer scale.

Land this without cynicism: the same slide explains why retrieval, tool use, unit tests and human review help so much. They are all ways of importing an external semantics, which is the engineering response to Gödel's second theorem, and it works. The card connects it back: the check that cannot come from inside a formal system, or from inside a self-compiling compiler, cannot come from inside a model either. Same theorem, new instance.

What actually changed

Generation got cheap. Verification did not.

systems judging, training, writing systems
  • Producing a program, a proof sketch, a draft was expensive and therefore scarce. Scarcity did the filtering. Now production is nearly free, and Part IV's asymmetry is exposed: the checking side did not move.
  • Models are trained on text that models wrote. Models grade models. Models write the code that trains models. The judge and the candidate share the same blind spots.
  • "Is this code correct?" is a semantic property. Rice: no general decision procedure. "Is this system right about itself?" Gödel: no self-certificate. An explanation of its own output is more output.
  • So the two ends of the loop are not generation: saying what should be true and establishing that it is. Both are acts of meaning, and neither is more notation.

The payoff of Parts III and IV together. Recall the asymmetry — hard to find, easy to check — and notice that cheap generation does not change the price of checking; it only removes the scarcity that used to do some checking for free. Every safety story that assumes a sufficiently smart system could verify itself is refuted by a 1931 theorem, which is not despair: it says exactly where a check can come from, and it is not from inside the loop. Part VI names the thing that supplies it.

Part VI

Definition

Everything so far was true before the machine arrived and stays true after it. Now the answer to the title — and a specimen to run.

Signal that we are landing. Four slides: the loop with its one external part, the definition, the specimen, and the reframe. Then the close and the sources.

The loop

A generator supplies notation. The semantics has to come from somewhere else.

what the machine supplies · and what it does not

Prompt in English, and what comes back is plausible notation: fluent, confident, and Part V says nothing about whether it is true.

Two things can happen next. Unchecked, the answer is simply accepted — and then there is no semantics anywhere in the loop.

Checked, it meets a compiler, a test, a proof, a measurement — a semantics in Part III's exact sense — and is kept or sent back round. That gate is the one part of the loop the machine does not supply.

The same shape, again
Cantor's list needed an object built from outside it. Gödel's system needed a stronger one. Thompson's compiler needed a second compiler. The check is external every time — and what supplies it is a domain: languages, and their semantics, understood.

Walk the figure slowly. Step one: the loop everyone already runs — an English prompt, a generator, a plausible answer. Step two: the route with no check in it; the answer is accepted because it reads well, and Part V has just said what that is. Step three: the gate. A compiler, a test, a proof, a measurement — a semantics, in the exact sense of Part III — and the answer is either kept or sent back round as the next question. Then the box, which is the point of the slide: this is not a new observation about a new machine, it is the fourth appearance of one theorem. The list, the system, the compiler and now the generator each needed something from outside to be checked, and in every case the something is a language with a semantics that somebody understands. That is a description, not advice; the next slide names the field that studies it.

The definition

So — what is computer science?

Working definition
Computer science is the exact study of notation a machine can execute — what can be written down, what can be computed, what can be decided, and what can be afforded — and therefore of the gap, measured precisely, between notation and meaning.
Not about computers

The machine is where notation is forced to be exact, so the gap was noticed here first and measured here most sharply. But the theorems hold for any notation: a statute, a genome, a score, a model.

And not finishable

No final language (Cantor), no complete system (Gödel), no self-certificate, no decision procedure for meaning (Rice). The subject cannot be completed — a property of the subject, not a complaint about it.

Test it: a compiler, an operating system, a proof assistant, a language model — each is notation given a meaning by a machine, and each is bounded by Parts I to IV. None of them is outside the definition.

Deliver slowly and let it sit. Four verbs, one per Part: written down is Part II, computed and decided are Part III, afforded is Part IV, and Part I is the size of the space all four live in. Then the two cards. Not about computers: the machine is the instrument; the results are about notation as such, which is why a lawyer, a biologist and a musician meet the same theorems in their own material. Not finishable: this is the property that distinguishes the subject from a body of facts, and it follows from the theorems rather than from anyone's ambition. Then test the definition against the room's own examples, ending with the language model, so it is clear that Part V was an instance and not a digression.

The specimen

One file that is the whole definition, small enough to read to the end.

built on selfie · applied to selfie
A language, and its meaning

C*: 7 keywords, one type and pointers to it. starc gives it a semantics — and starc is written in C*, so the dictionary is written in the language it defines.

A machine

RISC-U: 14 instructions, 32 registers, 4 GB. mipster interprets it — one universal machine, a page of C per instruction group, running every program for that machine, itself included.

A fixed point, and its limit

Selfie compiles itself and gets the same bytes twice — proof that it agrees with itself, and Thompson says what that cannot prove. So the tools around it turn its execution into logic, and are applied to selfie and to themselves: the outside checks, built in.

Read the specimen against the definition, card by card. A language with a semantics given by a compiler written in that language, which is Part III's first slide made runnable. A machine small enough to list on one slide and still universal, which is Turing's forward move. A fixed point that proves exactly one thing — self-agreement — and the tools that supply the outside check Thompson demands: symbolic execution, model checking, a fuzzer, a binary translator, each applied to selfie and to itself. The figure is that workshop. The only argument for the size is the one made in Part I: an artefact you can read to the end is qualitatively different from one you can only sample, because reading to the end is the only way the semantics is ever actually pinned down.

Reframe

Truth can only be approximated. The approximating never stops.

What the theorems forbid

A final language. A complete system. A machine that certifies itself. A decision procedure for meaning. A search that reaches every state, or an energy budget that could pay for one.

What they leave open

Every notation not yet invented, every truth not yet proved, every behaviour not yet written down — an unbounded frontier in a subject that, by its own theorems, cannot be finished.

The halting problem, applied to the subject itself:
this computation does not terminate.

The emotional summit, and still a statement of fact. What the theorems actually deny is redundancy: undecidable does not mean unknowable, it means unfinishable. Say plainly that a finishable subject would be one where the last question has a date, and that the five results in this talk are the proof that this one does not.

Close

Notation is finite.
Meaning is not.
The gap is the subject.

Computer science is the field that measured it — not to close it, which four theorems say cannot be done, but to say exactly where it is.

That is the whole answer. One slide left: the sources, and the specimen, ready to run.

Close on the definition, not on the audience. Three lines, then the one sentence that says what the field does with the gap: locate it, exactly, rather than pretend it can be closed. Then the sources slide, and questions.
