The question · and why we won't answer it

"Is it intelligent?" is a badly posed question.

It asks about a hidden property of a system, in a language — English — with no precise semantics for the word intelligent.

Psychology met this first and answered it best: you cannot measure a construct, only an operational definition of it — and the definition is never the thing. A century of psychometrics is a century of taking that gap seriously.

We will spend the next hour earning the right to ask a better question, one that stays valid no matter what technology arrives next.

The question of whether machines can think is about as relevant as the question of whether submarines can swim. Edsger W. Dijkstra, 1984

Dijkstra's point isn't dismissive — it's linguistic. "Swim" was coined for bodies with muscles. Submarines cross oceans; whether that is "swimming" is a question about our word, not about the submarine. Same for "think." So we replace the noun question ("is it intelligent?") with a process question, which we'll build up to.

The short answer · stated now, earned later

Intelligence is finding the next language — which means finding truth you cannot yet prove.

  1. Intelligence develops new formal languages — or at least new properties, expressible and provable in languages we already have.
  2. That requires finding and understanding promising unproven truth. You apprehend something as true while no proof exists; the notation is built afterwards, to hold what you already saw.
  3. Nothing hands you that step. No rule, no procedure, no quantity of compute derives it — a result we will prove, not assert. So it is a challenge for a first-year student, for a Nobel laureate, and for any AI, equally.

The rest of the hour is the derivation — and the reason this is good news for everyone in this room, whatever you are studying.

Give the answer away immediately; the talk becomes a derivation rather than a mystery. The load-bearing word is unproven: intelligence lives in the gap between truth and proof, which is exactly the gap Parts II and III measure. Note the symmetry — this is not a claim that humans have special sauce, it is a claim that the step is not algorithmic for anybody.

Part I

Vastness

First a feeling for size — then the smallest possible distinction, and everything it builds.

Part I is arithmetic only. No theorems. The goal is a physical feeling for state space, because everything later — bugs, search, learning, AI — happens inside one. The first four slides buy that feeling: the map, the anchors, the units, and the two meanings of "kilo". Nobody follows an argument about 1080 who has not first been handed somewhere to stand.

The map

One line carries the whole talk: small, vast, then two sizes of endless.

log scale · then off the end of every scale

Left. From one thing to every atom there is — the whole physical world in eighty steps, each one ten times the last.

The wall. No number of steps crosses it. Infinity is not the far end of the line; it is what the line never reaches.

Right. Past the wall, endlessness comes in sizes: one counts every notation, the other every meaning — and is strictly bigger.

The itinerary of the entire lecture in one picture, and worth a slow minute. Walk the sweep left to right: everything physics has to offer sits inside those eighty decades. Then the wall — and this is the sentence to land — infinity is not a very large number, it is not on the line at all; no amount of walking gets you there, which is exactly why Part II needs an argument rather than a bigger example. Then the two boxes: countable and uncountable, drawn deliberately at different sizes. Do not prove anything here, just plant the shape. Parts I and IV are claims about the left of this line, Parts II and III about the right, and every claim in the talk is a claim about where something sits on it. Hence the next three slides: a number you cannot place is a number you cannot argue with.

Anchors

A million, a billion, a trillion. Three words one syllable apart — three different worlds.

106 sa million seconds · 11½ days
109 sa billion seconds · 32 years
1012 sa trillion seconds · 32,000 years

A million seconds ago you had not yet packed for today. A billion seconds ago you were a child, or not yet born. A trillion seconds ago somebody was painting the walls of the Chauvet cave.

Each step is three zeros — the same three zeros, three times over. The words rhyme, so the mind files them together and treats the gaps as small. The gaps are not small. They are the subject.

The habit

Never accept a number you cannot picture. Find the anchor — a fortnight, a career, an ice age — or admit you are repeating the number, not reading it.

The single most useful slide in Part I for a non-technical room, because it is not about computers at all. Let the three seconds figures land one at a time and give the room a beat after "thirty-two thousand years" — Chauvet is roughly that old, so a trillion seconds ago is Ice Age Europe. The point is linguistic as much as numerical: million, billion and trillion are near-homophones for three quantities with nothing in common, and our intuition is built for the words, not the things. Everything later in the talk — 1080, 2266, a 20-billion-digit number — is unusable unless the room has practised converting an exponent into a picture. Ask them for their own anchors; the ones they supply are the ones they keep.

Units

Kilo, mega, giga, tera: the same three zeros, again and again.

prefixfactorbytes · how much it holdshertz · how often it happens
kilo103KB — a long paragraphkHz — the top of human hearing, 20 kHz
mega106MB — a novelMHz — the first IBM PC, 4.77 MHz, 1981
giga109GB — a thousand novelsGHz — this laptop; Wi-Fi at 2.4
tera1012TB — a million novelsTHz — infrared light

A byte is eight bits — one keystroke. A hertz is one beat per second. The left column counts things, the right counts events, and the prefixes do not care which.

Hold a nanosecond

At 1 GHz a beat lasts a billionth of a second, and light gets 30 cm in that time. Grace Hopper handed those 30 cm out as lengths of wire, so a room could feel why a signal cannot cross a desk and return inside one beat.

Two ladders, one set of rungs. Build the table row by row and read the right-hand column aloud — hearing tops out in the kilohertz, the first PC ran in the megahertz, this machine runs in the gigahertz, and light itself is up in the terahertz. That is a thousandfold three times in one line of vocabulary. Then the byte and the hertz: an amount and a rate, sharing prefixes, which is exactly why people mix them up in the wild ("a 5G phone" is a rate, "a 256 GB phone" is an amount). The Hopper wire is the slide's keepsake and the reason to say it out loud: she gave audiences a physical object whose length was a unit of time, which is the whole method of this Part in one prop. It also plants Part IV: at these rates distance is delay, and no cleverness removes it.

Two kilos

Kilo means a thousand. In memory it means 1,024. Both are in daily use.

base 10base 2gap
kiloKB = 103KiB = 210 = 1,024+2.4%
megaMB = 106MiB = 220+4.9%
gigaGB = 109GiB = 230+7.4%
teraTB = 1012TiB = 240+10.0%

Memory is addressed with bits, so its natural steps are powers of two — and 210 lands so close to 1,000 that the prefix was simply borrowed. Every further rung widens the gap.

Which one you were handed depends on the trade. Memory comes in powers of two; disks and networks in powers of ten — a "1 TB" drive holds 1012 bytes and shows up as 931 GiB. Hertz is never base two, and neither is the kilo in kilometre.

Notice what just happened

One notation, two meanings, and nothing in the notation to tell you which you were handed. Keep hold of that. The rest of the lecture is about that gap.

Short, practical, and it does real work later. The 1998 IEC names — kibi, mebi, gibi, tebi — are the fix nobody adopted, so both conventions remain live and you have to know which is on the label. The lived version everybody recognises: you buy a terabyte drive and the machine reports 931 gigabytes, and no one has stolen anything; two units share one word. Note that the drift compounds — 2.4% at kilo, 10% at tera, 12.6% at peta — so it is a rounding error at the bottom of the ladder and a lawsuit at the top. The last card is the reason this slide is in a talk about intelligence rather than in a hardware course: the string "GB" does not determine its own referent, and no amount of staring at the string settles it. That is the syntax-semantics gap in miniature, met on day one, in a shop.

One bit

A bit is one distinction: this, not that.

state space = 2ⁿ

Add a bit and you don't add states. You double them.

Ten bits: a thousand states. Twenty: a million. Thirty: a billion.

Doubling is the most underestimated operation in human reasoning — and the entire engine of computing.

Advance through the bit row and let the counter run. Emphasise: every distinction a system can make is a bit, and the *combinations* multiply. Ask the room to guess how many bits it takes to have more states than the universe has atoms. Answers usually land in the millions or billions. Then the next slide.

Scale check

34 bytes of memory has more states than the observable universe has atoms.

1080atoms in the observable universe
2266states of 266 bits ≈ 1.2 × 1080
37bytes to beat every photon, ~1089
log scale · 1 → 10⁸⁰

266 bits — thirty-four bytes, a short sentence, one cryptographic key — already exceeds the atom count of everything we can see. This is why cryptography works at all, and it is the first hint that the spaces we build inside computers are not merely large; they are of a different order than physical reality.

Your pocket

A phone with 8 GB of memory has 268,719,476,736 states.

Written out in decimal, that number has about 20.7 billion digits.

At 3,000 digits a page: 6.9 million pages. Bound into 500-page books: 13,800 volumes — some 400 metres of shelf.

That shelf does not hold the phone's states. It holds the number of them, written down once.

Every app you will ever install, every photo you will ever take, and every bug you will ever hit is one point in this space.

Land this slowly. The comparison people expect is "bigger than the universe." The truth is worse: the universe's atom count is a 81-digit number; the phone's state count is a 20-billion-digit number. There is no physical analogy left. We have built objects whose descriptions exceed the describable. If anyone asks about the units: 8 GB of memory is 8 GiB, exactly as the two-kilos slide said — memory is the base-two case — so the bit count is a round 236, and the exponent on screen is not a typo.

The consequence

In a space that big, good states and bad states look alike.

tested states, lit

Test a billion states per second, starting at the Big Bang, and by now you would have checked about 288 of them.

Out of 268,719,476,736. The fraction is not small. It is indistinguishable from zero.

Testing shows the presence, not the absence of bugs.Edsger W. Dijkstra, 1969

This reframes software bugs. Bugs are not evidence of sloppy people; they are the geometry of the space. No amount of diligence lets you visit the states. So engineering cannot be exhaustive search — it must be argument, structure, and abstraction. That is the seed of Part III.

Reframe · 1 of 5

The vastness is not the problem. It is the inventory.

What it forbids

Certainty by inspection. You will never check your system exhaustively — not with more testers, not with faster machines, not ever.

What it opens

Every symphony not yet written, every protein not yet folded, every proof not yet found, every program not yet imagined — all of them are already in there, waiting to be addressed.

The same enormity that hides your bugs is the reason novelty is inexhaustible. You cannot have one without the other.

First appearance of the recurring device: forbids / opens. Every limit in this talk gets this treatment. Make the trade explicit — a space small enough to audit would be a space too small to be interesting.

Interlude · literature

Borges built this space in 1941 and called it a library.

The Library of Babel holds every possible book: every truth, every refutation — and overwhelmingly, gibberish.

Its inhabitants go mad: containing every truth is worthless without a way of finding one.

Modern echo: a large language model already "contains" astonishing amounts of text. Containment was never the hard part.

From storage to search: from having the answer somewhere to knowing it when you see it.

Bridge slide, and a gift to the humanities students in the room. Borges gives us the vocabulary for the AI discussion at the end: a system that contains everything, including every plausible falsehood, has moved the whole difficulty into retrieval and verification.

Part II

Infinity

Vast is still finite. Now we count things that aren't — and discover that some infinities are bigger than others.

Warn the room: this Part is the only genuinely mathematical stretch of the talk, and it is the load-bearing one. Two of its slides are a worked proof, run twice on two different kinds of object, and they are worth the minutes. Everything after — Gödel, Turing, hallucination, alignment — is this one argument wearing different clothes.

Counting without numbers

Two sets are the same size if you can pair them up.

No counting needed — just a perfect pairing. Every left has exactly one right, and nothing is left over.

So there are as many even numbers as numbers: pair n with 2n. The part is as big as the whole.

A set pairable with 1, 2, 3, … is called countable. It can be listed.

n ↔ 2n

Galileo noticed the paradox; Cantor turned it into a definition. Stress the operational meaning of "countable" = "can be put in a list, first, second, third." That word does all the work in the rest of this Part.

Notation

Everything we can write down is countable.

A program is a finite string of symbols. So is a proof, a specification, a sentence, a score, a formula, a prompt.

List all strings of length 1, then length 2, then 3 … every finite text appears at some finite position.

So: all programs that will ever exist form a countable list. Same for all proofs. Same for all sentences of English.

the enumeration of all texts
This has a name

Assigning a number to each piece of syntax is Gödelisierung — Gödel numbering. It is how a machine reads a machine, and we return to it in Part III.

Key move: notation is countable — not because notation is small, but because it is finite-and-discrete. The audience should feel this as generous: countable is already infinite, we are not being cheap. And name Gödelisierung here so it is familiar later.

Meaning

What we want to talk about is not.

Consider all infinite sequences of bits: 0110100011… forever.

Each one is a complete answer to an endless list of yes/no questions — a behaviour, a function, a real number, a fate.

Claim: no list can contain them all. Not a long list. Not an infinite list. No list.

Cantor's proof is three lines and it is the most consequential argument of the 20th century.

the diagonal

State the claim and let the figure give the shape of the argument, but do not prove it here — the next four slides run it twice, slowly, on two different kinds of object. What matters now is that the room understands what is being claimed: not "the list would be very long", but "there is no list".

The objects

A subset of the numbers is one yes-or-no answer per number.

Take any collection of counting numbers — the evens, the primes, just {3}, none of them, all of them. Each one is a subset.

To pin one down, walk 1, 2, 3, … and answer in or out, forever. That answer sheet is an infinite bit string, and every infinite bit string is one.

All of them together make the power set, written 2ℕ — two choices, made ℕ times.

A handful have names. Almost all are an endless coin-flip, with nothing shorter to say about them than the flips.

subsets as answer sheets · 1 = in

The bridge slide, and the one to go slowly on: everything after this depends on the room seeing that a subset and an infinite bit string are the same object seen twice. Read the rows out — the empty set says no to everything, its opposite says yes to everything, the evens alternate, the primes have no pattern anyone can predict but do have a rule, {3} says yes exactly once. Then the last row, which has no rule and no name, and which is what nearly every subset looks like: to specify it you must transmit the answers themselves, forever. That is the first appearance of incompressibility, and it is worth naming as such if the room is technical.

The proof · 1 of 2 · subsets

Hand me a list of every subset. I will build one that is not on it.

● in · ○ out

Suppose the subsets could be listed: S1, S2, S3, …, every single one of them somewhere on the list.

Go down the diagonal and ask each row about its own number. Is 1 in S1? Is 2 in S2? Is n in Sn?

Now build D by answering the opposite every time: D = { n : n is not in Sn }. Nothing exotic — a rule anyone can apply, one number at a time.

D is not S1: they disagree about 1. Not S2: they disagree about 2. Not Sn for any n whatsoever. So the list left something out — and it was any list at all.

Take a full minute. Build the grid, ring the diagonal, then construct D one column at a time out loud: "1 is in S1, so 1 stays out of D; 2 is out of S2, so 2 goes into D." Then the kill: ask the room where D is on the list. Somebody will say "further down" — that is the moment to point at row n and ask what D and Sn say about the number n. They disagree by construction, at exactly one place, and that is enough. Stress that no assumption was made about the list: it was arbitrary, so every list fails, so no list exists. Students who have seen a proof by contradiction before will recognise the shape; students who have not have just seen their first, and it is one of the good ones.

The proof · 2 of 2 · numbers

The same move, on the numbers between 0 and 1.

Suppose you could list them: r1, r2, r3, …, each written out as an endless decimal.

Take the first digit of the first, the second digit of the second, the n-th of the n-th. The diagonal again.

Build a new number x by changing every one of those digits. Then x differs from r1 in the first place, from r2 in the second, from rn in the n-th — so x is on no row, and the list was not a list of all of them.

The one place it needs care

Change each digit to 5, or to 4 if it was already 5. That keeps you clear of the trailing nines: 0.4999… and 0.5000… are the same number written twice, and a proof that only landed on that would have proved nothing.

any list · and the number it missed

Run it exactly as before so the room hears the repetition — that is the teaching point, one argument in two costumes. The rows on screen are an arbitrary list, not famous constants; say so, because someone will try to recognise them. The care about trailing nines is worth the twenty seconds: it is the only crack in the proof, every serious textbook patches it, and showing the patch is what separates a proof from a slogan. If a mathematician in the room objects that this also needs the reals to have decimal expansions at all, agree — that is a construction of the reals, and it is upstream of us.

Intuition

Between any two numbers, however close, there is another.

halve, and halve again

Pick two reals as close together as you like. Their midpoint lies strictly between them. Now take those two — and do it again, forever.

There is no next real number: nothing to step from and nothing to step to. A list is nothing but firsts and nexts, which is the wrong shape for a line with no grain.

But do not let it do the work

Density is the feeling, not the reason. The fractions are dense in the same way — between any two there is another — and they can still be listed, by walking a grid of numerators and denominators corner by corner. Only the diagonal tells the two cases apart.

This is the slide that stops the room from believing the wrong thing for the right reason. The density picture is genuinely useful — it is why the line feels full and why "next" is meaningless on it — and almost everyone who meets uncountability first meets it as this feeling. But the feeling is not the proof, and the counterexample is one they already know: the rationals are dense and countable, so density cannot be what makes the reals uncountable. If you want the room to keep one methodological habit from Part II, it is this one: when an intuition and a proof agree, check which of them you are actually relying on.

Theorem

The diagonal, once and for all.

Cantor · 1891
For every set S, the set of all subsets of S is strictly larger than S. In particular, the real numbers cannot be listed.
Where self-reference enters

The proof builds an object from the list that asks of each row: "do you contain yourself here?" — and then answers the opposite. The list is used against itself.

You have just seen it twice

Once on subsets, once on decimals — and it was the same proof both times, because a subset is an infinite bit string and so is a decimal expansion. Power set, real numbers, bit sequences: three costumes, one cardinality. The theorem says it for every S at once.

Russell would turn the same trick on set theory itself in 1901: the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Same diagonal, aimed at the foundations.

Make the pattern explicit and name it, because it recurs three more times: (1) assume a complete list; (2) build a diagonal object by asking each item about itself; (3) flip. Cantor, Russell, Gödel, Turing — one idea. Students who see the pattern once never need to memorise the theorems.

The result everything hangs on

There are incomparably more meanings than notations.

countable notation · uncountable meaning

Programs: countable. Behaviours: uncountable. So almost every behaviour has no program.

Proofs: countable. Truths about numbers: uncountable. So almost every truth has no proof.

Sentences: countable. Distinctions the world admits: uncountable. So almost everything is unsaid.

"Almost every" here is exact: the expressible is a vanishing sliver, of measure zero, inside the meaningful.

This is the thesis slide of the whole lecture. Say it three ways, slowly. Note that we have not yet used any logic or computability — pure counting already tells us that notation can never catch up with meaning. Gödel and Turing will only make it concrete and constructive.

Reframe · 2 of 5

Scarcity of notation is a job description.

What it forbids

A final language. No vocabulary — mathematical, legal, musical, or neural — will ever cover the space of meanings.

What it opens

An inexhaustible supply of things worth naming. Every new notation captures meaning that was previously unreachable — and there is always more left.

Calculus, double-entry bookkeeping, staff notation, the periodic table, chemical formulae, DNA sequencing, type systems: each was a raid on the uncountable. The supply of raids never runs out.

Turn the cardinality gap into optimism. Chaitin's version: almost all numbers are random, i.e. incompressible, i.e. nameless. That is not a wall; it is unclaimed territory, and the act of claiming it is what every discipline calls progress.

Part III

Self-Reference

Counting told us the gap exists. Self-reference walks us to the edge of it and points.

Part II was static — a size comparison. Part III is constructive: we will not just know that unnameable things exist, we will write down specific questions no notation can answer. The instrument is always the same: a system turned on itself.

Syntax and semantics

The score is not the music.

Syntax · notation

Marks on paper. Finite, discrete, checkable, copyable. 1 + 1, a staff of crotchets, H₂O, a line of code, this sentence.

Semantics · meaning

What the marks are about. The number two. Sound in a room. A molecule that dissolves salt. A machine's behaviour over all inputs.

The map

A semantics is a function from notation to meaning. Defining that function precisely is the founding act of every exact discipline.

Frege 1892 · Sinn / Bedeutung Saussure · signifier / signified Korzybski 1931 · the map is not the territory Magritte 1929 · ceci n'est pas une pipe Peirce · sign / object / interpretant

Use the musicians in the room. A score under-determines the performance; two conductors give you two pieces. Yet the score is what can be printed, posted, copied and criticised. Notation travels; meaning has to be reconstructed at the other end. Every discipline listed on the chips discovered this independently.

Two kinds of language

Natural language is compression. Formal language is commitment.

"I saw the man with the telescope." Two readings, one string. English resolves it with a shared world you and I already have.

That shared world is why English is powerful — and why it cannot be a foundation. Its semantics is us, and we differ.

A formal language pays a price — narrowness, pedantry, effort — to buy one thing: a meaning that does not depend on who is reading.

naturalformal
ambiguoussingle-valued
elastic, forgivingbrittle, exact
persuadesproves
needs a mindneeds a machine

Prompting an AI in English is negotiation. Writing a test, a type, a schema, a unit, a contract is legislation.

Do not let formal win a beauty contest — natural language is the more remarkable artefact. The point is division of labour: you cannot compute with a poem, and you cannot fall in love in first-order logic. Progress happens when someone moves a piece of the world from the left column to the right. The last line previews Part VII.

Gödelisierung

Give every text a number, and notation becomes something you can compute with.

Gödel's device, 1931: encode formulas, proofs and programs as integers. Now arithmetic can talk about arithmetic.

Every computer you have ever used is this idea in metal: code is data. A compiler eats programs. An operating system runs programs. A model is trained on programs.

And once a system can describe systems, it can describe itself. Self-reference is not a trick; it is the price of being expressive enough to be useful.

Aside · for the computer scientists

In selfie, a 12KLOC C* system, a compiler compiles its own source and an emulator executes that code — including itself. Gödelisierung you can run in a terminal.

text → number → text

Stress the ordinariness: von Neumann's stored-program architecture is Gödel numbering as hardware. Every student in the room uses self-reference daily without naming it. That is why the theorems that follow are not exotic — they are the invoice for expressiveness we all want.

Theorem

There are truths with no proof.

Gödel · first incompleteness · 1931
Any consistent formal system rich enough to describe arithmetic contains statements that are true but not provable within it.
How

Build, by Gödelisierung, a sentence that says: "this sentence has no proof in this system." If it were provable, the system proves a falsehood. So it is unprovable — and therefore true.

The same diagonal

Cantor built a row not on the list. Gödel builds a truth not on the list of provable things. Adding it as a new axiom does not help: the construction simply runs again.

Proof is a finite object you can check. Truth is not.
Proof is syntax. Truth is semantics. They are not the same size.

The heart of the talk. Note what is NOT claimed: no single statement is forever unknowable — you may prove it in a stronger system. What is claimed is that no system is ever finished. Also note Gödel's own reading: he took this as evidence that mathematical insight is not exhausted by any fixed mechanism.

Theorem

And no strong system can certify itself.

Gödel · second incompleteness · 1931
Such a system cannot prove its own consistency — unless it is inconsistent, in which case it proves everything.
Read it as engineering

A trustworthy system cannot be the source of its own trust. Confidence has to come from outside: a stronger theory, an experiment, an independent auditor, reality.

Read it as advice

When a model explains why its own answer is correct, you have received more output from the same system — fluent, plausible, and not a certificate. Introspection is not audit.

Tarski, 1936, closes the circle: no sufficiently expressive language can define truth for its own sentences. Truth always lives one level up.

This slide is where the AI section gets its backbone; plant it deliberately. Self-certification is structurally impossible, not merely difficult — so any safety story that relies on a system vouching for itself is broken before you look at the details.

Undecidability

Will this program ever stop? No program can always tell.

feed the decider its own description

Suppose H(P,x) decides, for every program and input, whether P halts on x.

Build D(P): ask H whether P halts on P — then do the opposite.

Now run D(D). It halts exactly if it doesn't. Contradiction. So H never existed.

Turing, 1936 — the same paper that defined the universal machine, and thus invented the computer. The limit and the machine arrived together.

Walk the animation: H, then the wrapper D, then the self-application. Emphasise the historical detail — Turing was answering Hilbert's decision problem in the negative, and the byproduct of the negative answer was the design of the machine on every desk. Limits are where technology comes from. Do not land the gloom: the very next slide turns the same move over and shows what it builds.

The same move, forwards

One machine that can be any machine.

the job moved from the wiring to the tape

A machine used to be its job — to sort instead of add you built a different machine. Turing's move: put the machine's description on the tape, as data.

Then one machine U reads any description and does whatever that machine would do. That is universality: one piece of hardware, every possible behaviour, because the behaviour arrives as notation.

Your phone is not phone-shaped. It is U holding a description — which is why a new app needs no new hardware.

One sentence, read twice

Hand a decider its own description: contradiction, no H. Hand a machine any description: every program at once, one U. Turing published both in 1936.

This is the slide that stops Part III reading as a funeral. The move that produced the contradiction — a machine handed a description of a machine — is the identical move that produces the computer, and Turing made both in one paper. Say what universality actually buys, in the room's own terms: nobody in this lecture theatre owns a calculator, a typewriter, a map and a record player, because they own one object that becomes any of those when handed a description. That is not a metaphor for the theorem, it is the theorem being sold in a shop. Two things worth adding if the room is with you. First, any universal machine can simulate any other, so "computable" does not depend on which machine you picked — that is the Church-Turing thesis, and it is why this is a claim about the world rather than about one design. Second, universality is exactly what makes Rice's theorem bite on the next slide: a machine that can be any machine can have any behaviour, so there is nothing general left to decide. The gift and the limit are the same fact. Flag forward to Part V, where a ribosome turns out to be a universal constructor made of chemistry.

The general case

It is not just halting. It is every interesting question about meaning.

Rice · 1953
Every non-trivial property of the behaviour of programs is undecidable. Only questions about their text are safe.
Decidable · about syntax

How long is the code? Does it parse? Does it use this library? Are the types consistent? Does it contain a loop?

Undecidable · about semantics

Is it correct? Is it equivalent to that one? Does it ever leak the key? Is it free of infinite loops? Is it safe?

So every practical tool — a type checker, a test suite, a linter, a model checker, a fuzzer, a proof assistant — is a deliberate approximation: sound but incomplete, or complete but unsound, or exact only within a bound. Choosing which to give up is the discipline.

Rice generalises Turing and is the single most useful theorem for reasoning about AI evaluation. "Is this system safe" is a semantic property of a program. There is no general decision procedure — not for a lack of cleverness, but as a matter of mathematics. Hence: bounds, approximations, institutions. And the next slide shows that one profession has been living with exactly this for two thousand years.

Interlude · law

No text contains its own application.

Hart's core, and Hart's penumbra

A statute says no vehicles in the park. A car, plainly. And then the arguing starts.

Hart called the easy cases the core, the arguable ones the penumbra. No redrafting removes it — only moves it. The words are finite; the situations are not.

So law stops trying to settle meaning in the text and builds an institution instead: courts, appeals, precedent. Not a workaround — the only available design.

Gödel's other theorem

At his 1947 citizenship hearing, Einstein beside him, Gödel announced a self-referential flaw by which the Constitution could be legally turned into a dictatorship. The judge steered him off it, and nobody recorded which flaw he meant.

This slide is here for the lawyers, and for everybody else it is Rice's theorem with no mathematics in it. Do the vehicles bit as a show of hands — car, yes; ambulance, hesitation; bicycle, an argument; the war memorial jeep, laughter, and that is Lon Fuller's 1958 reply to Hart. The point to land: the penumbra is not sloppy drafting, and a smarter legislature does not remove it. Finite text, unbounded situations, so interpretation is structural — which is exactly the previous slide with wigs on. Then the constructive half, which matters more than the joke: law is the oldest working example of the response this whole talk recommends. It cannot decide meaning in the text, so it does not pretend to; it builds courts, appeals and precedent, an institution whose job is to approximate a semantics that no document can contain. Every field arrives here eventually. Computer science calls it code review, type checking and testing; medicine calls it trials and boards; science calls it peer review. On Gödel: the anecdote is well attested through Morgenstern's memoir, but the defect he had in mind was never recorded, so do not invent one — hand it to the room as an open question and enjoy that the man who found incompleteness in arithmetic went looking for it in a constitution.

Reframe · 3 of 5

No final authority means no ceiling.

What it forbids

A machine, a method, or a person that settles all questions. No complete rulebook, no self-certifying system, no automatic correctness.

What it opens

Mathematics is not a finished building but an open frontier; engineering is a craft rather than a lookup; and judgement — yours — never becomes redundant.

Beware of bugs in the above code; I have only proved it correct, not tried it. Donald E. Knuth, 1977

Knuth's joke is the whole lecture in one line: a proof is about a model of the world, and the world gets the final word. Proof and testing are two different approximations of truth, and a serious engineer uses both while trusting neither completely.

Part IV

Cost

Suppose a question is decidable. You still have to pay for the answer — in time, space, and energy.

Shift register: Parts II and III were about the possible. Part IV is about the affordable — and for practical purposes the boundary between "impossible" and "unaffordable" hardly matters. This is also where physics enters the talk.

Decidable ≠ doable

A question with a guaranteed answer you will never receive.

Given a logical formula over 100 yes/no variables: is there an assignment making it true? Perfectly decidable — try all 2100.

2100≈ 1.3 × 1030 assignments
1013 yearsat a billion checks per second

Chess has ~1044 legal positions; Go, ~10170. Nobody "solves" these. We navigate them — with heuristics, structure, and luck.

2ⁿ branches · one satisfying leaf

Note that modern SAT solvers routinely handle millions of variables on structured industrial instances — the exponential is a worst case, and defeating typical cases is one of engineering's great quiet victories. That is itself a hopeful data point: hardness in the worst case leaves enormous room for cleverness in practice.

Intractability

Hard to find. Easy to check.

That asymmetry has a name: NP — problems whose solutions are quick to verify even if finding them seems to need exponential search.

Cook and Levin, 1971–73: thousands of such problems are the same problem in disguise. Crack one efficiently and you crack scheduling, routing, folding, packing, proving.

Whether that is possible — P = NP? — is the most consequential open question in the exact sciences. Most researchers bet no.

The asymmetry, everywhere

Composing a symphony versus hearing it is off. Writing a proof versus reading it. Designing a protein versus assaying it. Doing the homework versus grading it.

Remember this one

Generation is expensive; verification is cheap. Every healthy division of labour — and every safe way to use an AI — is built on that gap.

This is the pivot slide for the practical advice at the end. Do not rush it. The asymmetry is what makes delegation rational: you can accept work you could not have produced, provided you can check it. When you cannot check it, delegation is faith.

Physics

Computation is not abstract. It costs energy, by law.

joules · log scale

Landauer, 1961: erasing one bit at room temperature costs at least kT ln 2 ≈ 3 × 10-21 joules. Information is physical.

So brute-forcing our 266-bit space costs ≈ 1059 J — about 1015 times everything the Sun will radiate in its entire lifetime.

Meanwhile a human brain runs on 20 watts: a dim light bulb, doing what data centres cannot.

Time, space, energy — three currencies for one budget. Any claim about intelligence that ignores the budget is a claim about magic.

The brain comparison is the honest hopeful note: nature found an architecture that is astoundingly cheap. That is not mysticism, it is an existence proof — dramatic efficiency gains remain available, because one implementation already achieves them. Bremermann's limit, 1962, is the general form of this ceiling.

Reframe · 4 of 5

Hardness is load-bearing.

What it forbids

Brute force as a strategy. You cannot search your way to correctness, to a cure, or to a business plan.

What it opens

Every private message, every signature, every payment, every password you rely on today exists because some problems are hard. Difficulty is the raw material of security — and the reason abstraction, theory, and taste are worth more than compute.

If exhaustive search worked, there would be no cryptography, no privacy, and — since anything findable would be found — nothing left to discover.

Make the point that a world where P = NP would be spectacular and also strange: mathematics automated, cryptography dead. The universe we appear to live in trades away that power for a currency called difficulty, which we then spend on privacy and on discovery.

Metrics

A language without a metric is decoration.

Notation lets you say it. Semantics fixes what it means. A metric tells you whether you are getting closer.

Running time, memory, energy, error rate, coverage, p-values, yield, latency, mortality, loss on a held-out set — every mature field runs on invented measures.

And every metric decays the moment it becomes a target.

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Goodhart's law · 1975

Teaching to the test. Optimising engagement. Publishing to the h-index. Training to the benchmark. In 1989 the CAST trial found drugs that suppressed the arrhythmia and raised the death rate. Same failure, five fields. A metric is a semantics for "better" — always approximate, and sometimes fatally so.

Metrics complete the triple that the final definition rests on: language, semantics, metric. Goodhart is the reason the third item needs constant reinvention — every good measure is eventually gamed, which means measurement, too, is an unending process rather than a solved problem.

Part V

Everywhere

None of this is about computers. Computers are just where it was noticed first, because there the notation is forced to be exact.

Explicit invitation to the non-computer-scientists: for the next four slides the examples come from their fields. The claim is strong — state space, syntax/semantics, self-reference, intractability and metrics are field-independent structure.

Biology

Life was running a formal language long before we invented one.

Syntax: 3.2 billion base pairs, two bits each — about 800 megabytes. Smaller than a phone's memory; state space 43,200,000,000.

Semantics: the genetic code maps 64 codons onto 20 amino acids and a stop — a redundant, many-to-one interpretation function, executed by the ribosome.

Self-reference: the genome encodes the machinery that reads the genome. Von Neumann described this architecture in 1948 — five years before Watson and Crick.

Intractability · Levinthal 1969

A modest protein has ~10300 possible shapes. Sampling them all would outlast the universe; real proteins fold in milliseconds. Nature does not search — it funnels.

And where AI actually won

AlphaFold did not solve folding by exhaustion either. It learned an approximation, and its value was settled by a metric the field had already invented and by experiments it could not fake.

Evolution is the great example of progress with proof entirely absent: no organism proves it is fit, it merely survives — truth by approximation, over deep time, with a brutal metric. And Levinthal's paradox is the biological statement of "intractable yet done daily," which is the most encouraging shape a hard problem can have.

Biology · self-reference

A copier that never interprets, and an interpreter that prints the copier and itself!

von Neumann's architecture, as run by a cell

Copy. Polymerase walks the strand and duplicates it letter by letter, never asking what a letter means. Notation to notation.

Interpret. The ribosome walks the same strand and executes it — an interpreter in the sense a 3D printer is one: description in, object out. Notation to meaning.

Close it. Among the objects it prints are the polymerase and the ribosome — and one ribosome prints any protein, because which protein is data on the strand. That is U from Part III: universality, in chemistry.

Why two tools and not one is the question to put to the room, and it is the deep content of the slide: obey the description only and the offspring inherits machinery but no description, so it could never reproduce in turn; copy it only and there is a text with nothing to run it. Reproduction needs the same string used both ways — once as instructions, once as inert data. Advance the figure a step at a time and narrate the two heads separately, because the whole point is that they do different things to the same letters. The polymerase is pure syntax: it would copy nonsense just as happily, and that indifference is why it is fast and why proofreading is even possible. The ribosome is the interpreter — and press the word "interpreter" in its strong sense, the sense in which a 3D printer is one. It does not read the strand and report on it; it executes the strand and a physical object comes out the other end. Three letters in, one amino acid out, the genetic code as the interpretation function, and a protein as the printed result. Then the closing step — the proteins coming off the ribosome include the polymerase and the ribosome themselves, so the description describes its own machinery. Von Neumann derived this in 1948 from pure logic, before the double helix, and the reason he needed both a copier and a constructor is worth stating slowly: a machine that only interprets its description builds a childless child. This is the same loop as the compiler that compiles itself and the emulator that executes itself from Part III, and the same loop Gödel builds a sentence out of. Collect on the universal machine explicitly: one ribosome prints any protein because which protein is data on the strand, so a ribosome is U built out of chemistry. Turing did not invent universality in 1936 — he noticed it, about a billion years late. If the room takes one thing from Part V, make it this: the syntax-semantics distinction is not a computer-science convention. It is load-bearing structure in something four billion years old and currently running in every cell of every person here.

Mathematics · physics · chemistry

The postulate nobody could prove was a door, not a wall.

one line, one point beside it · how many parallels?

Euclid's fifth: through a point beside a line there is exactly one parallel. For two thousand years everybody failed to prove it.

In the 1820s Bolyai and Lobachevsky changed the postulate instead. Many parallels, or none — nothing breaks.

Unprovable because independent: a choice, not a fact. Gödel's shape, a century early — and the way out was a new language, not more effort.

And then the notation knew first

Riemann built curved-space geometry in 1854 for nothing in particular; Einstein could not have written relativity without it. Mendeleev left holes in his table and named elements nobody had seen. Dirac's equation had a solution nobody wanted; the positron turned up in 1932.

The slide for the mathematicians and the physicists, and the strongest single answer to "what is a limit good for". Walk the figure: flat, sphere, saddle — on a globe every great circle meets every other, so there are no parallels at all; on a saddle there is room for many. Nothing needs to be proved here, only seen. Then the three beats. First: unprovable did not mean false, it meant independent, which is exactly what Gödel would later show is unavoidable in any strong system — Bolyai and Lobachevsky met the phenomenon ninety years before it had a name. Second: the response that worked was not more effort at the proof, it was inventing a new language in which the question changes. That is the thesis of this talk, and it is the only slide where the room can watch it happen historically. Third, and this is the one for the physicists: Riemann built the general machinery in 1854 for no application whatsoever, and in 1915 Einstein could not have written general relativity without it. Wigner called this the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics and did not explain it, and neither will we. If a mathematician asks about the parallel postulate versus Gödel — yes, independence from a specific axiom set is not incompleteness, and the honest statement is that they are the same shape rather than the same theorem. That is the claim on the slide.

Psychology · space and time

Working memory holds about four things — and what counts as a thing is up to you.

FBICIANASAUSB13 items · read once, look away, repeat
FBICIANASAUSB4 items · the same 13 letters, in the same order
Space bound · Miller 1956

Seven items, plus or minus two; later work says four. That number never grows. But an item is one symbol of a language you already own: a chess master shown a real position for five seconds puts nearly every piece back — shown a random board, barely better than a beginner.

Cowan 2001 · Chase & Simon 1973
Time bound · Kahneman 2011

System 1 answers from what is already stored: instant, cheap, plausible — and sometimes wrong. System 2 derives the answer: slow, expensive, checkable. Part IV's cost problem inside one head — a lookup against a proof.

System 1 / System 2
Way out · Piaget, Harnad

Input fits the scheme you have? Assimilate. Does not fit? Accommodate — rebuild the scheme. A new property, or a new language: the definition this talk ends on, run by a child. And a symbol is worth only what it is grounded in.

accommodation · symbol grounding 1990

The four slots never grow. What grows is what one slot can mean — that is what growing up is, and what a discipline is, run on one person.

The slide for the psychologists — and, read as a computer scientist, the mind as a machine with the two bounds every machine has: space and time. Run the demo before saying anything. Show the first row, ask the room to read it once, look away and repeat it; almost nobody can, thirteen is far past the limit. Show the second row; everybody can. Nothing on the page changed — the same thirteen letters in the same order — so the difference is entirely in the reader: whoever knows what FBI, CIA, NASA and USB are sees four things where the first row had thirteen. That is a chunk, and it is the thesis of this talk sitting in the audience's own heads: the notation is fixed and finite, what a symbol means is not, and the whole gain came from the meaning side. Anyone who does not know one of the acronyms sees three letters where you see one — the chunk is in the reader, not on the page, which is exactly the point. Then the three cards. Space: Miller's seven plus or minus two, revised down to about four by Cowan. The count is a hardware constant and never grows — on top of 86 billion neurons and around 10¹⁴ synapses, the front of the mind holds four things. But the unit is a symbol from a language you already own, and Chase and Simon's chess result is the cleanest evidence: a master shown a real game position for five seconds puts nearly every piece back, a beginner manages four or five; shown a random arrangement of the same pieces, the master is barely better than the beginner. Same registers, tens of thousands more patterns to point them at. Say it explicitly: this is what depth is, and the depth slide near the end cashes it in — depth is not a bigger memory, it is a bigger vocabulary. Time: Kahneman's two systems are, in our terms, a lookup against a derivation. System 1 returns an answer already stored — cheap, instant, and only plausible; System 2 computes one — slow, expensive, and checkable. That is Part IV's cost problem inside a single head, and it is the plausible-versus-provable split of Part VI a few slides early: a fast answer that feels right and is wrong is the same object as a hallucination. Way out: Piaget's pair. Assimilation is the case where the language you have still describes the input; accommodation is the case where it does not and the language itself has to change — a new property, or a new language, which is the definition this talk ends on, run by a child rather than by a science. Kuhn's paradigm shift is the same event at the scale of a field. Harnad supplies the other half: a symbol is worth only what it is grounded in, which returns in Part VI, where a model trained only on text has its symbols grounded in other symbols. Land the lead: nobody's slots grow; what grows is what a slot can mean, and that, run on one person, is what growing up is and what a discipline is.

Part VI

Machines

Now the question everyone actually came with. Today's AI is remarkable — and it is subject to every single thing we have just established.

Tone here matters. Not debunking, not hype. These systems are among the most useful artefacts ever built. And they are computations, so the mathematics applies to them exactly as it applies to a spreadsheet.

What it is

A large language model is a machine trained on notation, asked for meaning.

tokens in · a distribution over the next one out · then a sample

The name is exact. What a large language model — an LLM — is trained on is language, so the signal is syntactic: which symbol follows which. Meaning is never handed to it.

That this works as well as it does is the genuine surprise of the decade. It cost megawatts for weeks; you run on twenty watts. Neither figure changes what kind of object it is.

Billions of parameters, each many bits. Part I applies unchanged: that space cannot be inspected, so the behaviour cannot be enumerated. Only sampled.

Keep the description mechanical and unpolemical — students should leave able to explain what these systems are to a friend. The important consequence is the last one: "we tested it extensively" is Part I all over again. Testing shows presence, not absence.

The old gap, industrialised

A hallucination is a proof-shaped object that isn't true.

three tests · and they are not the same test

Fluency is syntax. Correctness is semantics. We built a machine of extraordinary fluency, so the gap between the two is something you now meet before breakfast.

Not a defect to be patched away: it is the proof/truth distinction at consumer scale.

So the durable response is not "trust it more" or "trust it less" but check it against something with a semantics — a compiler, a test, an experiment, a source, a colleague.

Grounding again

Harnad, 1990: symbols defined only by other symbols never touch the world. Tools and feedback plug that hole partially, never completely.

Land this without cynicism: the same slide explains why retrieval, tool use, unit tests and human review help so much. They are all ways of importing an external semantics. That is the engineering response to Gödel's second theorem, and it works.

Where it is going

World models aim at meaning itself — and inherit every limit anyway.

two routes to the same world

Not more text but a model of the world — state, dynamics, consequence. Predict what happens, not what is said: an attempt at real semantics.

And a bid for efficiency — fewer examples, fewer joules. On a fixed budget efficiency is capability, so world models may well outperform LLMs outright.

And still a finite notation, sitting inside the world it models. Parts II–IV apply unchanged.

What it inherits

Counting — models are countable, behaviours are not.
Rice — "is this model right?" is semantic. Undecidable.
Gödel — no self-certificate from inside.
Cost — the state space did not shrink. Only the map did.

Take the strongest version of the opposing case and then answer it. The world-model programme is not hype: it is the correct diagnosis of what is missing, and precisely what Harnad's grounding problem asks for — fix the interpretation by prediction against a world rather than by agreement with text. Do not skip the efficiency beat, it is the one with commercial teeth: text is a lossy, wildly redundant recording of a world, so learning from it is the expensive way round, and a system that learns the dynamics directly should need far less data, compute and energy for the same competence. Part IV's 20-watt brain is the standing proof that the job can be done orders of magnitude cheaper than we are doing it. Since every budget is finite, that is not a side benefit — it is how one approach overtakes another. Hence the honest forecast: LLMs are unlikely to be the last architecture. What no architecture escapes is Parts II to IV, because a model is still a finite object sitting inside the world it models. Better maps, arriving faster and cheaper, of a territory that stays uncountable — and still no map that certifies itself. If someone asks whether this changes the definition at the end, the answer is no: it is an example of it.

Self-reference · again

The loops are already closed.

systems judging, training, writing systems
  • Models are trained on text that models wrote — with measurable degradation when the loop tightens (model collapse, 2024).
  • Models grade models. The judge and the candidate share the same blind spots.
  • Models write the code that trains models, and agents invoke themselves.
  • "Is this system safe?" is a semantic property of a program. Rice says: no general decision procedure. Not hard — impossible.
  • And by Gödel's second theorem, no system this expressive can certify itself. An explanation of its own output is more output.

The payoff of Part III. Every AI-safety debate that assumes a sufficiently smart system could simply verify itself is refuted by a 1931 theorem. Which is not despair — it tells you exactly where to spend effort: on external, independent, bounded checks.

What actually changed

Generation got cheap. Verification did not.

Before

Producing a draft, a proof sketch, a program, an image, a translation was expensive and therefore scarce. Scarcity did our filtering for us.

Now

Production is nearly free and unbounded in volume. The filter has to be supplied deliberately — by specification, by measurement, by review.

Recall the asymmetry from Part IV: hard to find, easy to check.
That is a description of a healthy relationship with a machine.

Value migrates to the two ends AI does not occupy: deciding what should be true (specification) and establishing that it is (verification). Both are acts of meaning. Neither is automated by better generation — and the better generation gets, the more they are worth.

This is the most practically useful slide for students choosing what to learn. The advice is not "avoid AI" but "own the ends": be the person who can state precisely what is wanted and can tell whether it was delivered. Those skills appreciate as generation gets cheaper.

Part VII

Practice

A method that does not care which model is current, which company leads, or what happens next year.

Signal that we are landing. Everything from here is portable: a question, six habits, one definition. If the clock is tight, the six habits and the definition are the two slides that must survive.

The question, replaced

Stop asking whether it is intelligent.

Undecidable · unproductive

Is it intelligent? Does it truly understand? Is it conscious? Will it always behave? Each asks for a semantic verdict on a system, in a language with no semantics for the words used.

Decidable · today

What language did I state my requirement in? What are its semantics? What is my metric, and how will it be gamed? What is the budget? Who checks the result, from outside?

The right question is never about the machine's essence.
It is about your language, your semantics, your metric, your check.

This is why the method is future-proof: it never mentions a model, a vendor, or a year. The same five questions worked for the steam engine and the spreadsheet, and they will work for whatever arrives after transformers.

Explicitly answer the title of the talk's implicit promise: we are not going to define intelligence as a property because that is the badly-posed move. The definition as a process comes later in this Part, after the six habits and the bridge — and the process question is answerable, every day, by anyone.

Take this with you

Six habits with no expiry date.

  1. Name the language. State what you want in something with a semantics — a type, a schema, a unit, a test, an acceptance criterion. English negotiates; formality commits.
  2. Attach a metric, and name its failure. If you cannot say what "better" means, you are wandering. Then write down how the metric will be gamed — it will be.
  3. Delegate generation. Own verification. Accept work you could not have produced; never work you cannot check. That is the only shape delegation has ever had.
  4. Budget time, space, and energy. Know how big your space is and how much of it you can afford to visit. Most doomed projects were doomed arithmetically on day one.
  5. Break every loop from outside. Nothing certifies itself — not a model, not a company, not you. Import an independent check: an experiment, a proof, a person allowed to say no.
  6. Trust the unproven, then formalise it. When answers get hard to check, what is missing is a language, not more effort. Take the truth you can see but not yet prove — and build the notation that proves it.

Read each habit and give one example from the room's own disciplines. These six are the deliverable; everything before was the derivation. Tell students to photograph this slide.

Precedent

No theorem says your bridge will hold. You crossed one this morning.

Aviation, medicine, and civil engineering never had decidability either. Their questions are semantic, their state spaces astronomical, their proofs unavailable.

They became extraordinarily safe anyway — with layered approximations: standards, redundancy, staged testing, incident reporting, licensing, liability, and the freedom to say not yet.

None of it is proof. All of it is accumulated, measured approximation. It worked.

Therefore

"We cannot prove it safe" was never a reason for paralysis and never a licence for recklessness. It is the normal condition of every mature engineering discipline — and the reason those disciplines built institutions instead of theorems.

AI is early in exactly that process. Being early is a job opening, whatever you are studying.

The strongest available antidote to fatalism, and it is empirical rather than rhetorical. Commercial aviation is the clearest case: an undecidable safety property, driven to a few fatalities per billion passenger-kilometres by approximation and institutions. That is the template.

Back to the short answer

Every notation was invented by someone who saw a truth before it could be proved.

Fermat asserted it; Wiles proved it 358 years later. Riemann's hypothesis has been assumed true, and built upon, for 165 years. Mendeleev left blank cells for unseen elements. Darwin had no genetics.

In each case the notation came after the conviction — and the conviction was not deduced from anything.

Gödel makes this precise: no mechanical procedure yields the next axiom. Deciding what to hold true is not a step inside the system. It is a step to a new one.

Therefore, symmetrically

This step is not automatable — not because machines are weak, but because there is no algorithm there to automate. Scale does not help; it was never a compute problem.

The challenge for all of us

Machines are already formidable at proving within a language. The open frontier — for every student here and for every AI ever built — is finding promising unproven truth, and then building the language or the property that captures it.

This is the intuitive answer, now fully earned. Be careful and fair: the claim is not that machines cannot participate — they may well help, and models already propose conjectures. The claim is that no procedure guarantees the step, so it stays genuinely open work for whoever attempts it, human or machine. That is why it is the most durable skill anyone in the room can cultivate.

The definition

So — what is intelligence?

Working definition
Intelligence is developing new formal languages — or at least new properties in existing ones — which requires finding and understanding promising unproven truth. New languages and properties let us ask new questions about that truth, and then answer them in proofs. Forever.
Asking the right questions

Choosing which unproven truth is worth formalising. Not deducible, not teachable by rote — this is what advanced, graduate-level study is for.

Developing the skills to answer

Proving, computing, measuring, building. Rigorous, cumulative, teachable — this is what an undergraduate programme gives you.

Not a threshold, a score, or a possession — an activity with a direction and no terminating condition. And notice where the difficulty sits: not in the proving, which machines do superbly, but in the finding, which nothing yet does reliably.

Deliver slowly and let it sit. Two halves, deliberately mapped onto the room: undergraduates are acquiring the skills to answer questions; graduate study is where you learn to choose the questions. Both are necessary, and the second is the part no current or future system can hand you. Test the definition against a student learning statistics, a field adopting an instrument, an evolutionary lineage, a model being fine-tuned — all satisfy it, and none of it can be obsoleted by a product launch.

Why depth still matters

This is exactly why you study a field in depth.

Not because AI is unavailable — it is extravagantly available, and it will get better every year of your career.

But finding and understanding promising unproven truth requires having lived inside a subject long enough to feel where it is thin, where it is wrong, and where it is about to give.

No summary, no search, and no generated answer transfers that. It is built, slowly, and it is yours.

And it need not be computer science

Every field has unproven truth in front of it — history, medicine, ecology, music theory, economics, law. The method in this talk is field-independent; the depth has to be specific.

Pick the field — or fields — whose unsolved problems still make you strangely comfortable.

The career advice slide, and the honest answer to "why study anything if AI can do it?" Depth is the organ you find promising unproven truth with; it cannot be downloaded. End on the "strangely comfortable" line and pause — it is a better selection criterion than salary tables, and students remember it.

Reframe · 5 of 5

Truth can only be approximated. The approximating never stops.

What we lost

Completeness. Certainty. A final theory, a final language, a decision procedure for meaning, and any machine that could hand us all of it.

What we have instead

Work that cannot be finished, and therefore cannot be taken away: an unbounded frontier where every new notation makes yesterday's unreachable truths reachable, on every single day, forever.

The halting problem, applied to progress, gives the most hopeful result in science:
this computation does not terminate.

The emotional summit. Say plainly: a finishable world would be a world where curiosity has an end date. What the theorems actually deny us is redundancy. Undecidable does not mean unknowable — it means unfinishable, and unfinishable work is the only kind that never becomes obsolete.

Penultimate

Whatever intelligence is, humour is the only way.

For most of you, this hour has probably confirmed your conviction never to study computer science.

Or the exact opposite. And who knows — some of you may now be considering a switch into computer science. I have seen it happen. (With apologies to my colleagues in the other fields.)

Outcome A

Confirmed conviction.

Outcome B

Informed confusion.

Regardless of which one you leave with: this is about becoming who you are and no one else.

Deliver lightly, and mean the last line. The joke does real work: it gives everyone permission to have had either reaction, and then makes both reactions irrelevant to the actual message. Pause after "informed confusion" — it usually gets the biggest laugh of the hour — then land the final line quietly.

Denn es ist zuletzt doch nur der Geist, der jede Technik lebendig macht. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

For in the end it is only the Geist that brings any technique to life.

Geist — spirit, mind, meaning. For the last hour we have been calling it truth. And it is, finally, you and me.
Technik — technique, technology, the formal machinery. Necessary. Never sufficient. Never alive on its own.

Say the German first, then the translation, then stop. This is the whole talk in one sentence written long before any of the mathematics: Technik is syntax — notation, machinery, proof, the AI. Geist is semantics — truth, meaning, judgement, us. Notation is finite; meaning is not; and the part that brings the machinery to life is not the machinery. Thank the room and take questions.
